How training data affect a 3D-⁠CNN surface-⁠code decoder

A neural decoder learns from simulated shots, so which shots to generate and what label each carries are choices. Keeping the shots that matching gets wrong sounds like a good one, and it made the decoder worse. Sampling at a higher error rate and plain label smoothing did the most.

Independent research. Computing resources and feedback from the CSDC Lab, Dongguk University. 2026.

Codes
Rotated surface-code memory experiments at distances 3, 5, and 7 under circuit-level noise, simulated in Stim.
Decoders
A 3D-CNN with 1.4 to 8.7 million parameters, trained on 104 to 4 × 106 shots and compared against minimum-weight perfect matching.
Outputs
A paper under review at ICCDE 2027, and code with the results of all seven experiments.

A surface-code decoder reads the outcomes of repeated stabilizer measurements and decides whether the logical qubit has flipped. Minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) is the standard way to do this. Neural decoders treat it as a prediction problem, and on real devices they can beat matching.

They learn from simulated data, and a lot of it. AlphaQubit was trained on up to 2 billion examples, and a recent benchmark across architectures found that the amount of data matters more than the architecture. Because the data are simulated, every shot is a choice: at which error rate to sample it, whether to keep it, and what label to give it. These choices get much less attention than the model.

I wanted to know which of them actually matter, and how much training data each one is worth.

Three questions about the data

I studied one family of 3D convolutional neural network (3D-CNN) decoders on surface-code memory experiments at distances 3, 5, and 7, with noise on every gate and measurement. Each result is compared with matching through the ratio of logical error rates (LER), LER / LERMWPM. A ratio of 1 means the decoder has caught up with matching.

The first question is which shots to sample. Target-rate sampling draws shots at the error rate the decoder will face. Higher-rate sampling draws them at β times that rate, so errors are more frequent. Failure-enriched sampling keeps every shot that matching gets wrong and fills the rest with shots it gets right. Stratified sampling resamples shots by how many detection events they contain.

The second is how many. Training sets ran from 104 to 4 × 106 shots.

The third is what label each shot carries. A hard label is the logical flip that actually happened in the simulation. A soft label instead estimates how likely a flip is, given what the decoder sees. At distance 3 that probability can be computed exactly. At larger distances it has to come from a teacher decoder: matching, or Tesseract, a near-optimal decoder that costs far more to run. At distance 7, labeling a million shots with Tesseract took 3.5 hours on 240 threads. Label smoothing needs no teacher. It simply turns every 0 and 1 into 0.05 and 0.95.

Results varied between seeds as much as some of the effects did, so methods are compared seed by seed, with 95% bootstrap intervals. Three results were then rerun as follow-up checks, each with its pass criterion fixed before the run: one to explain why two studies disagreed, one to repeat a single-seed result with eight seeds, and one to test why smoothing helps. That makes seven experiments in all.

Two cheap choices did the most

At distance 5, the clearest effects came from the training error rate and from label smoothing. Neither needs anything beyond the simulator.

With a million training shots, every β above 1 beat target-rate sampling in both seeds. At β = 2 the error rate was 35% lower, and β = 2 reached the error rate of target-rate sampling at a million shots with 2.56 times fewer shots. Keeping the failures went the other way. It raised the error rate by 36%, and at every training set size both of its seeds were worse than both target-rate seeds. Stratified sampling gave no clear gain.

Labels from Tesseract did as well as doubling the data. Label smoothing did as well as Tesseract without any teacher: paired over 16 seeds, its error rate was 1.00 times Tesseract’s, with an interval of 0.96 to 1.05. Against hard labels on the same data, it lowered the error rate by 25%, in all eight paired seeds.

Both choices are free. They need a simulator and nothing else.

Why keeping the failures backfires

Keeping the failures sounds like hard-example mining: show the decoder more of the shots it has to learn. The catch is that it changes how often failures appear. Matching fails on only a small share of shots, 1.41% in Figure 1. The enriched training set draws from a larger pool and keeps all of its failures, which make up about 7% of the set, but only about 19% of its successes. That multiplies the odds that a training shot is a failure by about 5.3.

A decoder that fits this data learns those odds. It overrules matching on a syndrome whenever matching is wrong there more than about 16% of the time. On real data it should overrule matching only when matching is wrong more than half the time, so every syndrome in between is decided wrongly.

A second likely cause is that many of the kept failures cannot be learned at all. On a separate set of a million shots at distance 5, Tesseract also failed on half of the shots that matching failed on. For shots like these, the most likely explanation of the syndrome points to the wrong answer.

Sampling at a higher error rate shifts the labels too, but through the noise itself rather than by picking shots by their outcome.

What this means for training a decoder

The data needed grows quickly with distance. Catching up with matching took about 105 shots at distance 3 and 2 to 3 million at distance 5, 17 to 29 times more. At distance 7 the decoder had not caught up with 4 million.

So for this decoder, I would sample above the target error rate once data is not scarce. At distance 5 it started to help between 105 and 3 × 105 shots. I would not select shots by whether a decoder fails on them. And I would try label smoothing before building a teacher. A teacher pays off when it adds information that hard labels lack: at distance 3, exact probabilities cut the data needed for small training sets by 6 to 8 times, which smoothing did not match. Tesseract, at a far higher cost, showed no difference I could detect from smoothing at distance 5.

The last lesson is about method. A best β chosen on one seed did not hold up with four seeds, and two of my studies disagreed because one trained for more steps. With 3 × 105 shots, a floor of 6000 steps made the error rate up to 1.5 times worse than 12 epochs. Data choices should be compared over several seeds and with a training schedule sized to the data.

Limitations

Everything here comes from one 3D-CNN on memory experiments with uniform circuit-level noise, at distances up to 7. The amounts of data depend on the training setup. Yan et al. report a 3D-CNN of the same size as my distance-7 model that beats matching at every distance up to 9 with 5 million shots, using dropout, larger batches, and about 100 epochs.

The comparison between smoothing and Tesseract used one training set per distance. At distance 7 all gains were small, and 8 of 52 soft-label runs ended far worse than the rest, with ratios near 40 against a median near 12. I could not attribute these runs to the soft labels.

I also do not know why smoothing helps. One idea is that sampling at a higher error rate adds label noise and smoothing counters it. A check at β = 1 was compatible with this but did not establish it.

Setup

The circuits are rotated surface-code memory experiments in the Z basis with d rounds of stabilizer measurement, generated by Stim. Noise is uniform: depolarizing noise of strength p after each Clifford gate and on data qubits before each round, and bit flips with probability p after reset and before measurement. The target error rates are 0.5% and 0.3%. They come from studies run on different hardware and are not pooled. Matching is PyMatching with weights from the detector error model, and the teacher is Tesseract, with beam 20 at d = 5 and 10 at d = 7.

The decoder takes detection events on a (t, x, y) grid of shape (d + 1)3. It has four 3 × 3 × 3 convolutional layers with 64 channels and GELU activations, a dense layer of 256 units, and one output logit, for 1.38, 3.87, and 8.72 million parameters at d = 3, 5, and 7. Training uses binary cross-entropy, AdamW with learning rate 10−3 and weight decay 10−4, a one-cycle schedule, and batch size 2048. Paired comparisons report the geometric mean of per-seed ratios with a 95% bootstrap interval from 20,000 resamples.
